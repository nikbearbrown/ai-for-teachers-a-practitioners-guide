# Chapter 1 — The AI Dividend: What Teachers Actually Get Back

**TL;DR.** Teachers who use generative AI tools at least weekly report saving about 5.9 hours a week — a real, measured, self-reported figure from a 2,232-teacher Gallup / Walton survey released in June 2025, not the 20-hour number you may have seen on LinkedIn. The chapter's job is to show you what that number actually means, where the bigger projected number comes from, and how to decide which six hours are yours.

---

## Learning objectives

After this chapter you should be able to:

- **Describe** the Gallup / Walton (2025) finding: 5.9 hours per week saved, among the weekly users inside the 60% of teachers who used AI at all, n = 2,232, RAND American Teacher Panel.
- **Distinguish** ad hoc time savings (~6 hours, measured) from systematic-deployment ceilings (~16–17 hours, projected) and name what makes one a finding and the other a hypothesis.
- **Apply** the task taxonomy (AI-appropriate / hybrid / human-required) to three specific tasks in your own workweek.
- **Evaluate** a specific AI time-savings claim — a vendor case study, a headline, a colleague's anecdote — against the standard the chapter develops.

## 1. Sunday at 11 p.m.

It is 11:04 p.m. on a Sunday in late October. A high school English teacher — call her composite-illustrative, because she isn't a documented individual, she is every English teacher I've talked to in the last two years — is on her dining room table with thirty essays on *The Great Gatsby*. She read them once on Saturday morning. She is reading them again because she does not trust herself to remember what she wrote on essay number eleven by the time she gets to essay number twenty-three. Her margin comments are mostly the same four notes in different handwriting. *Where is the evidence for this claim?* *Watch the comma splice.* *Push the analysis further.* *What does Daisy's voice "full of money" actually tell you?*

She is not slow. She is doing the work the job requires. Five minutes per essay times thirty essays is two and a half hours. Twice. Plus the time to re-read the rubric. Plus the time to make a decision about the borderline ones. Plus the time she will spend on Monday morning resolving the three students who will contest their grades.

She has a vague resentment of the entire enterprise. The notes she is writing are not the notes she would write if she had infinite time. The notes she would write — the specific question that pushes *this* student to see *this* gap in *her* argument — those notes happen only on essays one through eight, when she is fresh. By essay sixteen, she is producing competent feedback that does not particularly help anyone. Including her.

The following Sunday she tries something different. She drops the rubric into Claude with the first essay and asks for a first-draft feedback document that hits each rubric criterion. Four minutes. She reads the draft. It is structurally correct. Some of it is wrong for this student. She rewrites the wrong parts and keeps the rest. Total time per essay: about twelve minutes. About one-third of her previous pace. The notes she puts on essays sixteen through thirty are the kind of notes she used to be able to put only on essays one through eight.

She finishes at 9:40 p.m. She spends the recovered hour and twenty minutes re-reading the three essays that needed her most — the ones where the question is not whether the student can write but what is actually going on with this student — and writes the kind of note that takes her, on average, about fifteen minutes to write and that the student will reread for years.

That is the AI dividend. Not doing less. Doing the right things, with the time that delegation of the wrong things gives back.

## 2. What the dividend actually is

The phrase "AI dividend" is not mine. It is the Walton Family Foundation's framing in *Teaching for Tomorrow: Unlocking Six Weeks a Year With AI*, the Gallup-conducted survey of U.S. K–12 public school teachers released June 18, 2025.[^gallup] Fieldwork ran March 18 to April 11, 2025. The sample was 2,232 teachers drawn from the RAND American Teacher Panel — a probability-based, nationally representative panel of U.S. public school teachers.[^rand] Both numbers — the *n* and the panel — matter for the hostile-reader test. This is not a vendor survey of customers. It is a survey of teachers, period.

Here is what the survey actually found.

**Sixty percent** of U.S. public K–12 teachers reported using AI for any school-related task during the 2024–25 school year. About **30%** — call it three in ten — used AI **weekly**.
<!-- FACT-CHECK FLAG: UNVERIFIED — see factchecks/01-the-ai-dividend-assertions.md (Gallup reports 32%, not 30%) --> The 5.9-hours-per-week figure applies to the weekly users, not to the full 60% and not to all teachers.[^gallup] When the Walton Family Foundation says "six weeks a year," it is multiplying 5.9 hours per week by roughly 40 instructional weeks. That math is honest. It is also a particular slice: the average among the roughly one-third of teachers who have already crossed the learning curve, not the gain a teacher will see in week one.

Across the nine task categories the survey asked about, **60% to 84%** of teachers who tried AI for that task said it saved them time. Seven percent or fewer said AI made the task *take longer*.[^gallup] The savings concentrate in a predictable cluster: preparing to teach, making worksheets and activities, modifying materials for student needs, and administrative work. The Gallup-Walton report itself names individualized feedback, student relationships, and instructional moves as the most-cited reinvestments of recovered time.[^gallup]

I want to dwell on the word "dividend" for a second, because the framing carries weight the data alone does not. A dividend is not a windfall. It is a recurring return on a deliberate investment — in this case, the investment of learning a tool well enough that it pays back more than it costs to operate. Teachers in the survey's first weeks of AI use typically *spend* time before they save any. The 5.9 hours is what is left after that investment has been made. A reader who tries AI on Monday and concludes after week one that the dividend is fake is reading the wrong moment of the curve.

### Ad hoc vs. systematic — two different numbers, two different epistemic statuses

The most consequential move in this chapter is the distinction between two numbers you will see in the AI-for-teachers conversation.

**5.9 hours per week. Ad hoc use. Measured.** This is what teachers who reach for ChatGPT or Claude or Gemini when a task feels heavy, get help, and put the tool down, report saving on average. It is an emergent property of many small uncoordinated reaches. It is the number that has primary-source, nationally-representative, n = 2,232 evidence behind it. When this book says "six hours a week," it means this number.[^gallup]

**16.7 hours per week. Systematic deployment. Projected.** This is a different animal. It is what you might *project* a teacher could save if AI were integrated across every category of their work — lesson prep, grading, admin, differentiation, assessment design, data analysis, professional development — at plausible substitution rates. It is built by multiplying baseline hours by AI-substitutable fractions. It is not measured. No randomized trial has yet evaluated a teacher who systematically deployed AI across all seven categories with the phase gates in place and reported back how much time came home.

The pantry table for this chapter — the one I'm about to show you — looks like a finding but isn't. It is a projection with one externally anchored row.

| Task | Baseline weekly hours | AI reduction | Hours recovered | Source / status |
|------|----------------------|-------------|----------------|-----------------|
| Lesson preparation | 8.0 | 35% | 2.8 | Anchored: NFER/EEF 2024 RCT measured 31% on the targeted task[^nfer] |
| Grading and feedback | 7.5 | 60% | 4.5 | Judgment estimate |
| Administrative tasks | 5.0 | 50% | 2.5 | Judgment estimate |
| Differentiation | 3.5 | 60% | 2.1 | Judgment estimate |
| Quiz / assessment development | 3.0 | 50% | 1.5 | Judgment estimate |
| Data analysis | 2.5 | 60% | 1.5 | Judgment estimate |
| Professional development | 2.0 | 40% | 0.8 | Judgment estimate |
| **Total projected** | **31.5** | **53%** | **16.7** | Projection — not a measured ceiling |

*Sources: lesson-prep row anchored to NFER/EEF (2024). All other rows are author estimates built on plausible substitution rates; treat them as hypotheses for your own workweek, not findings.*

Here is what is actually happening in that table. The lesson-preparation row has an external anchor: the NFER / Education Endowment Foundation randomized trial that ran during summer 2024 with 259 Year 7 and 8 science teachers across 68 English schools. The ChatGPT group spent 56.2 minutes per week on lesson prep; the control group spent 81.5 minutes. The difference is 25.3 minutes, or about 31% of the baseline.[^nfer] That is a measured number from a published RCT. It is approximately what the 35% in the table claims. The other six rows are not anchored to anything as solid. They are my best estimates of plausible substitution rates given what AI tools can do in each task category — informed estimates, but estimates.

This matters for two reasons. First, the hostile-reader test. A teacher who hears "16.7 hours a week" and goes looking for the citation should find a row labeled "projection," not a row labeled "finding." Second, the calibration question. If a teacher plans her semester around 16 hours of recovered time and lands at 5, she will either burn out chasing the number or conclude AI is overhyped. The right thing to plan around is 5.9. The 16.7 is a ceiling that *might* be approachable with sustained workflow design, and the book's job, beginning here and continuing through Chapter 12, is to help you find out how close to it your particular week can get.

There is also a third number floating in the conversation that you should know about and not chase: **20 hours per week.** This is power-user testimony. It comes from teachers — myself included, in private conversation — who have invested months building tool-specific workflows, anonymization habits, prompt libraries, and the muscle memory to know which tool to reach for at which moment. It is achievable. It is not typical. The book anchors on 5.9 because 20 is a hypothesis about what is possible, not a description of what is happening. Mixing them in the same sentence damages the credibility of both.

### The reinvestment finding

The dividend question that matters most is not "how much time" but "what for." The Gallup-Walton report asks directly. Teachers who save time with AI most commonly report reinvesting it in **deeper instructional planning, more individualized student support, and relationship-building with students and families**.[^gallup] Frequent AI users in the same sample also report higher optimism about student engagement than non-users — a correlation, not a causal claim, but suggestive of where the time is going.[^gallup]

The pattern matters because it is the empirical anchor for the preface's argument. Recovering six hours and absorbing them into more grading produces nothing. Recovering six hours and using them on a one-on-one conference, a redesigned unit, or a parent email that goes beyond template is the dividend. The right things, not less.

The honesty caveat here: this is self-reported reinvestment. The teachers said they were using the time this way. The survey did not follow them around and measure where the hours actually went. The closest causal evidence we have on whether teacher AI use translates into improved student outcomes is still pending — the NFER follow-up trial of Oak National Academy's *Aila* lesson-planning tool is recruiting ~450 Key Stage 2 teachers in 86 English primary schools, with results expected in 2026.[^aila] Until then, the reinvestment finding is suggestive evidence, not closed evidence. Treat it accordingly.

### What other countries' teachers work

The dividend frame lands harder when you see the baseline it is measured against.

- **United States:** U.S. teachers regularly report 50+ working hours per week in national surveys.[^rand]
- **Australia:** OECD TALIS data put Australian lower-secondary teachers at about 45 hours per week. The Grattan Institute documents many Australian primary teachers working 50–55 hours, with a quarter spending 10+ hours per week on lesson preparation alone.[^grattan]
- **Finland:** OECD TALIS 2018 reports Finnish lower-secondary teachers at **31.6 hours total per week** — well below the OECD average, with about 10 hours combined for lesson planning, collaboration, and marking.[^talis]

Finland's number is not a typo. A country runs an excellent school system on substantially less teacher time. The 5.9-hour dividend in the U.S. context is partial structural correction — about 11% of total working time and a larger share of the discretionary hours that compete with sleep — toward conditions other education systems achieve through policy. AI is not the only path to those conditions. It is the path most U.S. teachers can take this Monday.

## 3. The task taxonomy: where to put each task

The chapter's central piece of machinery is the taxonomy: every recurring teacher task falls into one of three categories.

**AI-appropriate.** The cognitive work is preparatory rather than pedagogical. Generating a first draft of a worksheet. Producing six versions of a problem at varying difficulty. Summarizing a research article you will read closely but cannot read cold this morning. Writing the bones of a parent newsletter. Converting bullet notes into prose. The human judgment in these tasks is in *selection and editing*, not in *production*. AI is good at production. You are good at selection.

**Hybrid.** AI handles the bulk draft; human review is non-negotiable because the output reaches a student or a parent. Feedback on student work. IEP language. Lesson sequencing for a specific class. Behavioral incident reports. The phase gate (Chapter 2) lives here. The dividend is real on these tasks, but only after you have set the gate correctly. Hybrid tasks without phase gates produce a different and worse failure mode than refusing to delegate them at all.

**Human-required.** Tasks that cannot be delegated without breaking the educational function they serve. The live conversation responding to the actual confusion in the room. The relationship with a student losing momentum. The recognition of a misconception embedded in an almost-right answer. The modeling of what it looks like to not-know-yet and keep going. The judgment call no rubric can make.

This is structurally close to Ethan Mollick's centaur / cyborg / "just me" distinction in *Co-Intelligence*, but the test is different.[^mollick] Mollick's frame is about task efficiency and where AI fits cleanly into the workflow. This book's test is pedagogical: would removing your cognition from this task remove something the student needed? If yes, the task is human-required, no matter how efficient delegation would be.

The taxonomy is a *sorting question*, not a verdict. Two teachers grading essays may sort the activity differently. For one teacher, essay grading is mostly draft feedback — hybrid. For another, essay grading is the place she catches the misconception that decides tomorrow's lesson — human-required, even though the marginal comments could be delegated. The taxonomy works because it forces you to ask the question, not because it returns a fixed answer.

## 4. Worked example: one teacher, one week, the calculation

Let me show you the taxonomy doing work on one concrete week.

Take a middle school social studies teacher with 120 students across five sections. She is the same composite-illustrative teacher who sat at her dining room table with the *Gatsby* essays — different class, same week. Here is what her hours actually look like, by her own count, on a typical week in October.

| Task | Hours / week | Category | What gets recovered |
|------|-------------|----------|---------------------|
| Lesson planning (5 lessons × 5 sections, ~1.6 hrs each) | 8.0 | AI-appropriate for structure; human-required for class-specific judgment | ~2.8 hrs (35% of 8.0) |
| Grading and feedback (essays, document analyses, short answer) | 7.0 | Hybrid — AI first-pass, teacher review | ~4.2 hrs (60% of 7.0) |
| Parent communication and admin email | 4.0 | AI-appropriate for routine; human-required for sensitive | ~2.0 hrs (50% of 4.0) |
| Differentiation (Lexile, ELL scaffolds, IEP-aligned materials) | 3.0 | AI-appropriate with anonymization gate | ~1.8 hrs (60% of 3.0) |
| Quiz / assessment design | 2.5 | AI-appropriate for first draft; teacher selects items | ~1.25 hrs (50% of 2.5) |
| Data analysis (gradebook, attendance, formative data) | 2.0 | AI-appropriate with anonymization | ~1.2 hrs (60% of 2.0) |
| Live instruction | 25.0 | Human-required | 0 |
| Hallway / lunch / live supervision | 7.5 | Human-required | 0 |
| **Total** | **59.0** | | **~13.25 hrs projected** |

Walk through what the table is doing. The total work week is 59 hours — high but within the range for a U.S. middle school teacher with 120 students.[^rand] Live instruction and supervision are non-delegable; those 32.5 hours stay where they are. Of the remaining 26.5 hours of discretionary work, the projected substitution rates (drawn from the same table I told you not to fully trust) suggest about 13.25 hours could come home under systematic deployment with phase gates. That number is more than 5.9 and less than 16.7 — closer to the midpoint than the ceiling — and that is approximately where I would expect a careful teacher to land in year two of using these tools well.

Here is the lesson, in one sentence: **the dividend you actually get is the sum of the per-task substitutions you actually run, on your actual week, with the phase gates the tasks actually need.** Not an average. Not a projection. The number on your week.

And the limit, in one sentence: **the table assumes the phase gates from Chapter 2 are in place.** A teacher who delegates grading without the rubric calibration gate, or differentiation without the anonymization gate, will save time and produce harm. Hours come home with consequences attached.

## 5. Common misconceptions

The dividend conversation has accumulated four misconceptions that need addressing before the rest of the book is usable. For each one I will name the misconception, explain why it fails, and tell you what it would have done to our Sunday-night essay grader if she had believed it.

### Misconception 1: "AI will replace teachers."

The claim fails on the level the survey measures. AI saved 5.9 hours per week for weekly users. A U.S. teacher's week is 50+ hours. The dividend is 11% of total working time. The remaining 89% is live instruction, live supervision, relationship work, the catch on the almost-right answer, and the judgment calls a rubric cannot make. None of those are inside what AI does well.

The deeper failure is that "replacement" is the wrong question. The right question is "which tasks can be delegated such that the educational function the task serves is preserved." For some tasks the answer is many. For others it is none. The 5.9 hours sits in the first category. The remaining time sits in the second.

What would have happened on Sunday at 11 p.m. if our teacher had believed this misconception? She would either have refused to use the tool — and stayed up another two hours producing comments that didn't help — or she would have over-delegated, sending Claude's first draft directly to students and discovering on Tuesday morning that three of those drafts were wrong in ways only she could have caught. Both failures are real. The dividend lives between them.

### Misconception 2: "The time savings aren't real."

This is a different failure mode and it is widespread. The claim usually rests on three observations: that the savings are self-reported (true), that early use is slower than usual (true), and that some teachers don't see savings (true).

The response is not to dismiss any of these but to read them precisely. Self-reported time savings tend to *overstate* compared to measured savings on the same task; the NFER/EEF RCT measured about 25 minutes of saved lesson-prep time per week, while the Walton-Gallup self-report averages 5.9 hours across all tasks.[^gallup][^nfer] These numbers are not contradictory — different scopes, different methods — but a careful reader should expect the measured number on any single task to come in below the self-reported number. Early use is genuinely slower; the 5.9 hours describes weekly users who have already crossed the learning curve. And the teachers who don't see savings are usually using AI for tasks where it doesn't help much (live instruction; relationship work; the misconception catch) rather than the tasks where it helps a lot (drafts, modifications, summaries, communications).

If our Sunday-night teacher had believed this misconception, she would have read the Walton-Gallup number, decided the survey was vibes, and stayed at the dining room table.

### Misconception 3: "Twenty hours per week is the target."

This is the failure mode that worries me most because it comes from people who are correct in spirit. They have seen power users do remarkable things with AI. They have heard "20 hours" or "30 hours" or "I work two days a week now." They want the same. They plan their semester around it.

Twenty hours per week is real for some users. It is not what is happening on average. The Gallup-Walton sample says 5.9 hours for weekly users. The book anchors there because that is where the evidence actually lives. The 20-hour number is power-user testimony — achievable with sustained workflow design, multiple tools mapped onto specific tasks, and the kind of muscle memory that takes months to build. Treating it as the target sets the teacher up to fail twice: first by missing it, then by deciding AI didn't work because she missed it.

If our teacher had believed this misconception, she would have given Claude every essay on Friday night and gone to bed at a reasonable hour — and on Monday she would have stood in front of her class without having read essays sixteen through thirty closely enough to notice that two students had stopped writing about *Gatsby* and started writing about themselves. That is the difference between a 12-hour week and a 20-hour week that the 20-hour-headline doesn't tell you about.

### Misconception 4: "All time savings are equal."

The savings are not fungible. An hour recovered from drafting a worksheet is different from an hour recovered from grading. The first leaves you with a draft you need to review against your own knowledge of the class. The second leaves you with feedback that may or may not connect to what the student actually needs.

Bastani et al. (2025) ran the cleanest demonstration of the parallel failure on the student side. About 1,000 Turkish high school students used a general-purpose ChatGPT during math practice. They scored 48% higher than the control group during the practice session. On the subsequent unaided exam, they scored 17 percentage points lower.[^bastani]
<!-- FACT-CHECK FLAG: CONTRADICTED — see factchecks/01-the-ai-dividend-assertions.md (Bastani et al. report 17% relative reduction, not 17 percentage points absolute) --> The savings during practice were real. The cost on the exam was also real. The two numbers tell the same story: time saved without the right division of labor produces brittleness — outputs that look correct but don't survive the next test.

The teacher-side analog is direct. Delegate lesson preparation without phase gates and the lesson is "ready" but the teacher does not know it. She has not anticipated the misconceptions. She has not selected the worked example that matters for this class. She has saved an hour and lost the pre-load of contextual understanding that the hour was producing. The lesson runs anyway. The first three students who confuse cellular respiration with photosynthesis catch her flat. The hour came home; the teaching went with it.

If our Sunday-night grader had believed this misconception, she would have shipped Claude's drafts. Three borderline cases would have received feedback that was generic where it needed to be specific. One of those three would have walked into Tuesday's parent conference with a comment her father didn't understand. The dividend would be 4 hours and the cost would be 90 minutes of a parent conference plus the trust that comment cost her.

## 6. Exercises

### Exercise 1: The Workweek Audit (Apply)

Spend twenty minutes producing a table that looks like the one in §4, but for your own week. List every recurring task in a typical workweek. Estimate hours per week for each. For each task, write one of three labels:

- **AI-appropriate** if the cognitive work is preparatory and the output will not directly reach a student or parent without your review.
- **Hybrid** if the bulk draft can be delegated but a phase gate is required because the output reaches a student or parent.
- **Human-required** if the educational function depends on your cognition being inside the task.

Two rules:
1. Sort by the task you actually do, not the task in the job description.
2. If you don't know which category a task belongs in, that's the most interesting one. Flag it. We'll return to it in Chapter 2.

This audit will be the recurring anchor for Chapters 4 through 12.

### Exercise 2: The Specific Claim Audit (Evaluate)

Find one AI time-savings claim in the wild this week. A vendor case study. A LinkedIn post. A district email. A news headline.

Run it against four questions:

1. **Is there a primary source?** A measured number from a study, not a press release.
2. **Is the population specified?** Who, how many, what kind of teachers, in what country, when?
3. **Is the savings measured or projected?** Was it counted, or is it a multiplication of plausible substitution rates?
4. **Does the claim distinguish ad hoc from systematic use?** Or does it imply the average user gets the power-user number?

Write a one-paragraph assessment. If the claim fails any of the four questions, the claim is not yet ready for your planning. File it under "interesting; not actionable."

### Exercise 3: The Sunday Test (Create)

Pick the single most miserable task you did this past week — the task you did at the worst time of day, the task you resent. Write a paragraph describing exactly what cognitive work the task required. Then highlight, in two colors, the parts of that paragraph that only you could do versus the parts that any competent assistant could do given your instructions.

Re-read the highlighted-yellow parts. That is your phase gate for this task. You will operationalize it in Chapter 2. The fraction of the task that is highlighted other-than-yellow is, roughly, your AI-substitutable share for this task. The 35% in the table for lesson preparation is what the average of these fractions looks like across 259 secondary science teachers in England.[^nfer] Your number for your task will be different. That's the point.

### Exercise 4: The Reinvestment Pre-commitment (Apply — challenge)

This one is harder. Take the AI-appropriate and hybrid totals from your audit. Multiply them by 0.5 (a conservative substitution rate that approximates what most teachers see after they've cleared the learning curve). The result is your conservative projected dividend for next month.

Now, *before* you start saving any time, decide what you will do with those hours. Write three sentences:

1. The hours will be used for: [specific instructional or relationship investment, named at the level of "one-on-one conference with Marcus on his argument structure," not "more time for students"].
2. I will know I am using them as planned because: [observable evidence — a calendar block, a documented conference log, a parent email pattern].
3. If by the end of next month I find the hours absorbed by other tasks, I will: [a specific remediation, not a vague commitment].

The reinvestment trap — saved time getting absorbed back into the schedule — is the most common way the dividend disappears in practice. Pre-committing the hours in writing is the cheapest known fix.

## 7. What would change my mind

The chapter rests on the claim that the 5.9-hour figure is real and reproducible for teachers who use AI weekly, and that systematic deployment plausibly recovers more. Three findings would force a revision.

First, **a peer-reviewed RCT showing systematic AI deployment across multiple teacher task categories produces less than the ad hoc 5.9-hour figure**, after the learning curve. This would invert the projection — it would mean integration costs exceed integration gains — and the 16.7-hour ceiling would need to come down sharply, possibly below the ad hoc number. I would update by lowering all the substitution rates in the projection table to whatever the trial measured, and I would say so on the next edition's first page.

Second, **a measured reinvestment study showing that teachers' self-reported reinvestment of saved hours into instructional work overstates actual reinvestment by more than half** — that is, the hours are reported as instructional time but are actually absorbed by administrative creep, additional sections, or simply leisure. This would weaken the chapter's central pedagogical claim ("not doing less; doing the right things") into a claim about hours per se, and would require renegotiating the dividend frame.

Third, **NFER/EEF Aila trial results showing AI-assisted lesson prep produces measurably worse lesson quality** even as it reduces time — would require adding a quality-cost line to the table and revising downward the projected ceiling for the lesson-prep row in particular. The current trial design measures both time and resource quality; the 2026 result will be the closest thing we have to a definitive answer.[^aila]

## 8. Still puzzling

Four things this chapter does not resolve and that I am watching closely.

1. **The cross-country dividend.** The dividend evidence is overwhelmingly U.S., U.K., and Australian. Whether teachers in Finland, Singapore, Korea, or Japan get the same 5.9-hour return — or whether the systems that already minimize teacher workload leave less room for AI to recover — is unknown. The cross-country comparison in this chapter is workload, not dividend.

2. **The contract question.** Whether the dividend is achievable depends partly on labor relations and contract language that the chapter does not resolve. A teacher whose union contract counts lesson prep as in-classroom hours is in a different position from a teacher whose prep is donated time. The dividend lands on the prep hours either way; what changes is who captures the value.[^domain]

3. **The durability question.** The 2025 dividend was measured on the AI tool landscape of late 2024 and early 2025. Tools change. Prompt patterns that worked in March may not work in October. Whether the 5.9 hours is the floor as tools improve, or whether tool churn erodes the dividend by forcing repeated relearning, is open.

4. **The student-outcome question.** Time saved is established. Better student outcomes from the reinvested time is plausible but not yet causally demonstrated. The Aila trial begins to close this loop in 2026.[^aila]

The dividend is real. The ceiling is a hypothesis. The reinvestment is the test.

Chapter 2 introduces the phase gate — the explicit boundary between AI work and human work that makes the dividend safe to spend.
