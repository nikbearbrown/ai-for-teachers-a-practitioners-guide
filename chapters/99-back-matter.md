## Acknowledgments

This book exists because of the teachers — at Northeastern, at the districts I have worked with, in the Humanitarians AI fellowship, and in the AI Fluency program — who pushed back on early drafts and would not let me hide behind frameworks where they wanted prompts. They are the reason every chapter ends with something a teacher can use on Monday morning.

Three debts are larger than the rest. Nina Harris, my co-instructor in INFO 7375 (Branding and AI), shaped the chapters on communication and writing through years of arguing about voice, judgment, and where AI helps versus where it papers over. The Medhavi research team — particularly the colleagues working on the Genuine Learning Probability framework — kept the *Frictional* argument honest by insisting that *measurement* of learning is the test the book has to pass, not just *description* of it. The Humanitarians AI fellows, who built the tools and ran the pilots that made several of the worked examples real, deserve credit on every page they show up in.

Two researchers shaped the book from a distance. The Bastani et al. (2025) PNAS team produced the result that made the phase gate non-negotiable; Liang and colleagues at Stanford (2023) produced the evidence on AI detection bias that grounds Chapter 13 in equity rather than enforcement. Neither team is responsible for how I have used their work.

To the high schoolers who wrote, "My friends are using AI to write their papers but I want to use AI to learn and I don't know how" — Chapter 14 is the answer I owe you. To the teachers who read drafts at 11 p.m. on Sundays and told me which sentences would not survive contact with a real classroom — every sentence that did survive is yours as much as mine.

Errors that remain are mine. Sources I could not verify are flagged in the text. The book does not pretend to be finished; it pretends to be honest.

## Notes

These chapter-grouped endnotes supplement the in-line citations. Where a chapter cites a source the reader is likely to seek out (a primary RCT, a foundational paper, a specific data release), the source appears here with a note on why it matters and what to read first.

### Preface

1. **Bastani et al. 2025 — the load-bearing study.** "Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics" (Bastani, Bastani, Sungu, Ge, Kabakcı, Mariman), *PNAS* 2025, DOI [10.1073/pnas.2422633122](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122). Approximately 1,000 Turkish high schoolers in a math practice study with three arms — control, GPT Base (unrestricted), GPT Tutor (built with pedagogical guardrails). GPT Base outperformed control during practice (≈48% gain) but lost ground on the unassisted exam (−17 percentage points). GPT Tutor outperformed during practice (≈127% gain) and held its exam scores. The August 2025 correction (DOI [10.1073/pnas.2518204122](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2518204122)) is affiliation-only and non-substantive. Read the original. The result is the empirical anchor for the phase-gate argument.

### Chapter 1 — The AI Dividend

2. **Walton Family Foundation / Gallup 2025.** *Teaching for Tomorrow: 2025 Survey of K–12 Teachers* (June 18, 2025). n = 2,232 public-school teachers, RAND American Teacher Panel. Weekly AI users (about 32% of the 60% who use AI at all) report saving 5.9 hours per week. This is the 5.9-hour figure cited throughout. Note: the Walton/Gallup study is self-report; the NFER/EEF RCT below is task-measured.
3. **NFER / EEF Teacher Choices Trial (2024).** n = 259 secondary science teachers in 68 English schools. ChatGPT plus a brief guide reduced lesson-preparation time by 31% in the standard condition. The study measured time and resource quality on blind review, *not* student learning outcomes. Generalization beyond KS3 science is not established.
4. **OECD TALIS 2018.** Finland's lower-secondary teachers report 31.6 working hours per week. Australia's 2018 figure is 45 hours. The book uses TALIS as the cross-country comparison baseline rather than secondary aggregators.

### Chapter 2 — The Phase Gate

5. **Dell'Acqua et al. 2023.** "Navigating the Jagged Technological Frontier" — Harvard Business School working paper 24-013. The empirical basis for the claim that AI capability is non-monotonic across tasks. Read for the experimental design.
6. **Shulman 1986 / 1987.** "Those Who Understand: Knowledge Growth in Teaching" (*Educational Researcher*, 1986) and "Knowledge and Teaching: Foundations of the New Reform" (*Harvard Educational Review*, 1987). The foundational PCK papers. Read 1987 first.
7. **Hattie's effect sizes.** Cited for direction and rank, not exact magnitude. The d = 1.57 for collective teacher efficacy is contested; see Bergeron & Rivard (2017), Wrigley (2018), Kraft (2020) for the methodological critique.

### Chapter 3 — Prompting That Works

8. **Anthropic Prompt Engineering Guide** and **OpenAI Best Practices.** Current docs. Both age in months, not years; treat as living references.
9. **Wei et al. 2022.** "Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning in Large Language Models" — arXiv:2201.11903. The empirical basis for step-by-step prompting.
10. **Zheng et al. 2023.** "Helpful Assistant or Fruitful Facilitator? Investigating Personas in System Prompts" — arXiv:2311.10054. Persona prompting is mixed; small negative effects on factual benchmarks.

### Chapter 4 — Lesson Planning

### Chapter 5 — Assessment, Grading, and Feedback

13. **Steiss et al. 2024.** "Comparing the quality of human and ChatGPT feedback of students' writing." *Learning and Instruction* 91, 101894. ChatGPT-generated feedback was rated slightly lower than expert teacher feedback overall, with specific gaps in actionability and contextual fit.
14. **Henkel et al. 2024.** "Can Large Language Models Make the Grade? An Empirical Study Evaluating LLMs Ability to Mark Short Answer Questions in K-12 Education." arXiv:2405.02985. Comparative study of LLM short-answer marking accuracy against human raters (GPT-4 few-shot κ ≈ 0.70 vs. human-human κ ≈ 0.75).

## A note on the index — and why this book does not have one

This is a Kindle-first / online-first release. Both formats provide full-text search that does what a print index does, faster and more flexibly. For online readers, search-and-find replaces alphabetical lookup. For Kindle readers, Kindle's native search and X-Ray do the same work. Compiling and maintaining a print-grade index for a book whose primary medium is digital would add production cost without giving readers anything they cannot already do.

This book is also being integrated with **[Medhavi](https://www.medhavy.com/)** — *Medhavi AI* (also written *Medhavi*; from Sanskrit मेधावी, "intelligent" or "intellectually brilliant"). Medhavi is the AI-native adaptive tutoring and intelligent textbook platform that grounds several of the framework arguments in this book and that will eventually carry the live, queryable version of every chapter, exercise, and prompt template.

In the Medhavi version, the index is not a list. It is a conversation. You ask a question. The system finds the place in the book that answers it, surfaces the surrounding context, and — when the question is operational rather than encyclopedic — runs the relevant phase-gated workflow with you. The static index would be a worse version of that conversation.

For now: use search. For the live version: come learn something with us at [medhavy.com](https://www.medhavy.com/).

## Glossary

Short definitions of the key terms the book introduces or borrows. Each term is taught in its first-use chapter; this is a quick-reference.

**Ad hoc AI use.** Occasional, opportunistic use of AI tools without a systematic workflow. The Gallup/Walton 2025 reference category — associated with the floor of ~6 hours/week saved. See Chapter 1.

**AI-survivable assignment.** An assignment designed so that a student who uses AI freely cannot produce a passing artifact without doing the cognitive work the assignment is meant to surface. Five-question checklist in Chapter 13.

**Anonymization gate.** Phase Gate 12: PII removed before any external AI interaction. Protects FERPA, COPPA, and IDEA compliance. Principle-level only; defer specifics to district counsel. See Chapter 6 and Chapter 13.

**Assertion-evidence approach.** Alley's (2013) slide-design framework: the slide headline is a complete assertion (a claim with a subject and a verb), and the slide body is the visual evidence for that assertion. Contrasts with topic-label headlines. See Chapter 8.

**Backward design.** Wiggins & McTighe's (2005) lesson-design framework: start from the desired learning outcome, then design the assessment that would demonstrate it, then design the instruction that prepares for that assessment. See Chapter 4.

**Bastani finding.** The Bastani et al. (2025) PNAS result: students using unrestricted AI during practice scored 48% higher on practice and 17 percentage points lower on the unassisted exam, relative to controls. With pedagogical guardrails (GPT Tutor), practice gain rose to 127% and the exam decrement was eliminated. The empirical anchor for the phase-gate argument. See Preface and Chapters 2, 5, 10, 13, 14.

**Botspeak.** Nik's prompt-as-specification framework; the foundation for Chapters 3 and 12. See [bearbrown.co/method/botspeak](https://www.bearbrown.co/method/botspeak).

**Capability-building vs capability-borrowing.** The student-facing distinction (Chapter 14): AI use that *generates* the friction the learner must overcome (Socratic challenge, edge-of-competence practice, Feynman-test conversation) builds capability; AI use that *removes* the friction (writing the first draft, summarizing the reading, solving the problem) borrows it.

**Cognitive offloading.** Risko & Gilbert (2016) — the tendency to externalize cognitive work to a tool. In moderation, productive (calculators); in the wrong place, the mechanism by which the Bastani performance paradox occurs.

**Compared with what?** Cairo's (2016) honesty check on every quantitative claim: the claim is meaningless without an explicit reference. See Chapter 9.

**Five-question chart checklist.** The honesty diagnostic for a chart: (1) What question does the chart answer? (2) Compared with what? (3) Does the y-axis start at zero for a bar chart? (4) Can the reader read the ranking in 5 seconds? (5) Does the title state the finding, not just the subject? See Chapter 9 and Appendix C.

**Five-question slide diagnostic.** The honesty diagnostic for a slide: (1) Is the headline a claim or a topic label? (2) Would this slide teach without a speaker? (3) Is there a sentence on this slide I'm about to say aloud? (4) Does the visual form match the content structure? (5) Is every color encoding something? See Chapter 8 and Appendix C.

**Fluency trap.** The phenomenon in which smooth AI output produces false confidence — a learner *feels* like they understand because the artifact reads well, but storage strength has not increased. Related to Bjork's storage-vs-retrieval distinction. See Chapter 14.

**Frictional framework.** The book's theoretical spine: learning is a biological event triggered by cognitive friction; AI that removes struggle removes the trigger; AI that makes productive struggle more accessible enhances learning. Full treatment in Appendix G. See also [irreducibly.xyz](https://irreducibly.xyz).

**Genuine Learning Probability (GLP).** Medhavi's measurement counterpart to Frictional: a probabilistic assessment built from seven friction-trace components rather than from a single artifact score. See Chapter 13 and Appendix G.

**Jagged technological frontier.** Dell'Acqua et al. (2023): AI capability is non-monotonic — some tasks are unexpectedly easy for AI, others unexpectedly hard, and the boundary is irregular. See Chapter 2.

**Lexile framework.** MetaMetrics' readability metric. The chapter caveat: AI manipulates Lexile inputs (sentence length, word frequency) reliably and *conceptual* content unreliably. See Chapter 6.

**Pedagogical Content Knowledge (PCK).** Shulman (1986, 1987): the teacher's knowledge of *how* particular concepts are best taught to particular students, including which misconceptions students typically carry and which analogies typically work. The irreducibly human part of lesson design. See Chapter 4.

**Phase gate.** The explicit boundary between AI processing and human professional judgment for a specific task. Twelve gates organize the book. See Chapter 2 and Appendix A.

**Performance paradox.** The Bastani 2025 pattern: better performance during AI-assisted practice; worse performance on the unassisted assessment. See Chapter 13.

**Process documentation.** Required artifacts of the cognitive process (drafts, annotations, decision records) that survive AI use because they capture the *act* of producing, not just the *product*. Component of AI-survivable assignments. See Chapter 13.

**Prompt library.** A personal, organized collection of prompts that have worked, annotated for context and known failure modes. Treated as a knowledge asset (Davenport & Prusak). See Chapter 12 and Appendix B.

**Redundancy Principle.** Mayer's (CTML) finding: simultaneously presenting the same content in two modalities (e.g., text on a slide identical to the speaker's narration) degrades learning relative to one modality alone. See Chapter 8.

**Rubric calibration gate.** Phase Gate 1: run AI grading on five samples and compare to your own scores; adjust until consistent; only then process the bulk. See Chapter 5.

**Slideument.** Reynolds's (2008) term for a slide that tries to be both a speaker's projection anchor and a standalone document, and fails at both. The default product of AI slide generators. See Chapter 8.

**Storage strength vs retrieval strength.** Bjork's distinction: storage strength = durability of the memory; retrieval strength = current accessibility. The two can diverge, which is why fluent immediate performance is not evidence of durable learning. See Chapter 14.

**Systematic AI use.** Routinized, prompt-libraried, time-blocked AI use organized around a personal workflow. The projected (not yet measured) ceiling of ~16.7 hours/week saved. See Chapter 1 and Chapter 12.

**Task taxonomy.** The classification of teacher tasks into AI-appropriate, hybrid, and human-required. The decision framework for delegation. See Chapter 1.

**Worked example.** A step-by-step demonstration on the page of the cognitive process the chapter is teaching — including dead ends. The pedagogical move that distinguishes a textbook from a handbook. Every chapter has one.
