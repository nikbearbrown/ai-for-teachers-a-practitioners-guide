# Chapter 8 — Making Slides with AI

**TL;DR:** AI slide generators reliably produce decks that look professional and teach poorly, because their defaults reproduce the patterns of bad slides at scale. The fix is not a better tool — it is a five-question diagnostic the teacher can run on any deck in under five minutes, anchored to Mayer's Redundancy Principle and Alley's assertion-evidence structure.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

1. **(Understand)** Describe the slideument problem: a slide that tries to be both speaker's anchor and self-contained document fails at both.
2. **(Apply)** Distinguish between a claim headline and a topic label, and rewrite a topic label as a claim.
3. **(Apply)** Write a slide-generation prompt that specifies headline as assertion, body as visual evidence, notes field as speaker explanation.
4. **(Analyze)** Apply the Mayer Redundancy Principle: text on a slide that duplicates the speaker's narration degrades learning.
5. **(Evaluate)** Run the five-question diagnostic on any deck in under five minutes.
6. **(Create)** Iterate on an AI-generated deck using targeted follow-up prompts that name the specific failure and the specific fix.

**Prerequisites:** Chapter 2 (the phase gate) and Chapter 3 (prompt structure: role, context, task, constraints). The five-question diagnostic *is* the phase gate for slide acceptance, and the slide-revision prompts in this chapter use the structure taught in Chapter 3.

**Phase gate:** Teacher runs the five-question diagnostic on every AI-generated deck before accepting it as final.

## 1. The 11:42 p.m. deck

It is 11:42 p.m. on a Thursday. You have lecture at 9 a.m. The deck is forty-three slides long, generated by an AI tool you trust enough to use but not enough to ship without checking.

Slide twelve has a paragraph from chapter 6 of the textbook with one word bolded. Slide nineteen has a five-color bar chart of three categorical variables. Slide twenty-seven has a stock photo of a stethoscope on it, for reasons you cannot reconstruct. The slides look professional. They look done. They look wrong.

You try to fix slide twelve. You drag the text box left. The bolded word loses emphasis because the background isn't dark enough now. You change the background. The headline contrast drops below readable. You undo. You try again. You give up on slide twelve, move to slide nineteen, and within ninety seconds you have produced a different version of the same problem.

It is not 11:42 anymore. It is 12:38. The deck is no better.

The problem is not AI. The AI did its job — it produced a deck. The problem is the absence of a vocabulary for what is wrong. You can feel the wrongness. You cannot name it. And you cannot prompt the tool to fix what you cannot name.

This chapter builds the vocabulary. Five questions. Each one names a specific failure mode with a specific fix. By the end you can run them on any AI-generated deck in under five minutes and produce a follow-up prompt that the tool will execute precisely.

## 2. Concept I — The slideument problem

Garr Reynolds, in *Presentation Zen* (New Riders, 2008; 2nd ed. 2011), coined the word **slideument** to name what happens when a presenter tries to make one file do two incompatible jobs at once: serve as the speaker's visual anchor *and* serve as the audience's standalone study document. A slide is a slide. A document is a document. They are different artifacts with different density requirements. Trying to make one file be both produces something that fails at both jobs — too dense to lecture from cleanly, too poorly structured to study from efficiently.

Here is why the failure happens, and why it is invisible from the author's chair.

A **speaker's anchor** is a sparse artifact. It holds a claim, a diagram, a single striking number — just enough to tell the speaker what comes next and give the audience something to look at that does not compete with the explanation being delivered out loud. The explanation lives in the speaker's voice and the notes field. The slide gets out of the way.

A **study document** is a dense artifact. It holds enough prose to reconstruct the argument without anyone speaking. It might look like a textbook page. That is fine — if it is deployed as a textbook page, viewed in silence, where there is no narration for the verbal channel to conflict with.

The slideument is what you get when the author builds the dense artifact and then lectures from it. The slide carries the full explanation because the author wanted it to be usable for study. But then the speaker delivers the same explanation out loud. Now look at what just happened.

### Mayer's Redundancy Principle — the mechanism

The cognitive cost is not aesthetic. It is measurable. Richard Mayer's Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning (CTML), summarized in *The Cambridge Handbook of Multimedia Learning* (3rd ed., Cambridge University Press, 2021) [verify: page numbers for the Redundancy chapter in the 3rd edition], rests on three findings from cognitive science: humans have two processing channels (verbal and visual-spatial), each channel is capacity-limited, and learning requires active processing in each.

From these, the **Redundancy Principle**: adding on-screen text that duplicates the spoken narration degrades learning, because both streams compete for the same verbal channel.

The empirical anchor is Moreno and Mayer (2002), "Verbal Redundancy in Multimedia Learning: When Reading Helps Listening," *Journal of Educational Psychology* 94(1): 156–163 [verify page range]. In their lightning-formation experiment, participants who received narration plus animation outperformed those who received narration plus animation plus redundant on-screen text on both retention and transfer measures. The effect has been replicated; it can reverse when the on-screen text is reduced to brief labels close to the visual referent (Yue, Bjork & Bjork, 2013, *Journal of Educational Psychology*), but the default — verbatim or near-verbatim redundancy with narration — degrades learning.

Here is the practical translation. **If you are about to say a sentence aloud, that sentence does not belong on the slide.** Move it to the notes field.

The misconception worth naming: *putting the talking points on the slide helps students who can't follow audio.* This conflates two students. The one sitting in the room with the speaker is harmed by redundant text — the verbal-channel collision degrades the explanation she is trying to follow. The one studying alone later is helped by a separate document — which is what the populated notes field, exported, becomes. One slide cannot serve both students. Two artifacts produced from one source file can.

### Edward Tufte and the Columbia case

Edward Tufte's *The Cognitive Style of PowerPoint* (Graphics Press, 2003; 2nd ed. 2006) gave us much of the vocabulary for criticizing slides — "PowerPoint engineering," "chartjunk," the slide as a "low-resolution stupid format" for technical content. Tufte's analysis of the Columbia accident slides ([Columbia Accident Investigation Board Report, Vol. 1, Ch. 6](https://www.nasa.gov/columbia/home/CAIB_Vol1.html)) is the load-bearing case: NASA engineers' risk assessment of foam-strike damage was formatted as PowerPoint bullets, the hierarchy collapsed, the quantitative nuance disappeared into bullet indentation, and the people reviewing the slides did not have what they needed to assess the risk. Seven astronauts died.

Tufte is a vocabulary contributor. The empirical case for *most* of his prescriptions, however, rests on the Columbia exhibit plus his information-design priors — the controlled experiments are Mayer's and Sweller's, not Tufte's. We will use Tufte's terms; we will lean on Mayer's evidence when the load-bearing claim is empirical.

## 3. Concept II — Headlines that assert, not label

The second move was developed by Michael Alley at Penn State's College of Engineering and laid out in *The Craft of Scientific Presentations* (Springer, 2nd ed. 2013). Alley calls it **assertion-evidence**. The structure has two parts.

1. A **sentence headline** that states the slide's main message as a complete assertion.
2. **Visual evidence** — diagram, chart, photograph, equation, table — that supports the assertion.

No bullet lists. The default PowerPoint template encourages something different: a topic label at the top followed by indented bullets underneath. Alley calls this the **topic-subtopic** structure. It is what slide-editor defaults produce, what conference posters use to look "academic," and what most AI slide generators ship as their default style.

The empirical case for assertion-evidence: Alley, Schreiber, Ramsdell, and Muffo (2006), "How the Design of Headlines in Presentation Slides Affects Audience Retention," *Technical Communication* 53(2): 225–234 [verify pagination]. Audiences viewing assertion-evidence slides scored significantly higher on comprehension and recall than audiences viewing topic-subtopic slides on the same content. A second finding worth noting: student *presenters* who built assertion-evidence slides understood the content better than student presenters who built topic-subtopic slides on the same material. The slide structure shaped the presenter's own learning, not just the audience's.

Here is what the distinction looks like operationally:

| Topic label (the AI default) | Claim headline (the rewrite) |
|---|---|
| "Photosynthesis" | "Chloroplasts split water and capture light to make sugar." |
| "Three Causes" | "MBS leverage was the load-bearing cause." |
| "Methods" | "We compared treatment to control with a Mann-Whitney U test." |
| "Results" | "Reading scores rose 14% in the treatment group; no change in control." |
| "Introduction" | "Most teacher AI rollouts fail at the training step, not the tool." |

The label tells the audience what *region* of the discipline this slide is in. The claim tells the audience what to *believe* by the end of the slide. A claim is testable against the slide's evidence. A label is not testable against anything.

The misconception worth naming: *topic labels are more "professional" than full sentences.* The opposite is closer to the truth. Newspaper headlines are claims. Op-ed headlines are claims. Conference-poster headlines, in the assertion-evidence tradition, are claims. The "Introduction / Methods / Results / Discussion" label set is an academic-poster default, not a professional default. The teaching slide should follow the newspaper, not the unrefereed conference poster.

## 4. Concept III — The three text-density failure modes, the wrong visual form, and color that encodes

The slideument problem and the topic-label problem are the two structural failures. Three more failures show up at the slide-by-slide level. Each has a specific name and a specific fix.

### Redundancy — the prose on the slide

If a sentence on the slide is a sentence you are about to say aloud, you have triggered the Mayer Redundancy Principle. The fix is migration, not deletion: the sentence goes to the notes field, where it survives as part of the study artifact, and the slide body keeps only what the visual channel needs — a diagram, a label, a number.

### Seductive detail — the stethoscope on slide twenty-seven

Harp and Mayer (1998), "How seductive details do their damage: A theory of cognitive interest in science learning," *Journal of Educational Psychology* 90(3): 414–434, names the failure: interesting-but-irrelevant material added to instructional content reduces learning, even when audiences report enjoying the material more. The stock photo of the stethoscope on a slide about pharmacokinetics is a seductive detail. So is the cartoon of Einstein on a slide that has nothing to do with relativity. So is the "fun fact" sidebar that does not connect to the slide's claim.

The Coherence Principle, also from Mayer, prescribes the fix: cut decoration. If a visual is not doing instructional work, the visual is taking attention and giving nothing back. Delete it. If the slide feels bare, the slide was already bare; the photo was just hiding it.

### Density — the textbook paragraph

If the slide contains a paragraph of prose that the audience would need to read silently to follow, you have built a study document and are about to lecture over it. The fix is **segmenting**, another Mayer principle: break the dense content across multiple slides, each with one claim and one visual, or migrate the prose to notes and replace the body with a diagram. Whichever you choose, the slide body should never be a paragraph the audience is reading while you talk.

### The wrong visual form

A bulleted list is the slide editor's default for almost everything. It is the right form for, roughly, lists. It is the wrong form for comparisons (use a 2×2 or a table), for processes (use a diagram with directed arrows), for distributions (use a chart), for quantities (use a number set at large size). Cleveland and McGill (1984), "Graphical perception: Theory, experimentation, and application to the development of graphical methods," *Journal of the American Statistical Association* 79(387): 531–554, ranked visual encodings by how accurately the human eye perceives them: position along a common scale beats length beats angle beats area beats color. When a slide uses bullets to show a comparison, the audience has to translate the bullets into a mental table before they can compare. The translation is cognitive work the slide should have done.

### Color as encoding, not decoration

Color on a teaching slide should answer one of two questions: *what category is this?* (categorical encoding) or *how much of this?* (sequential or diverging encoding). If a color answers neither — if it is doing decoration — it is spending visual bandwidth on nothing.

The 11:42 p.m. deck's slide nineteen is the canonical failure: a five-color bar chart of three categorical variables. There are more colors than there are categories. The eye sees variation that does not encode meaning, hunts for the pattern, finds none, and disengages. The fix is to count the categories the chart needs to distinguish, then use exactly that many colors, chosen from a palette designed for distinguishability (Cynthia Brewer's [ColorBrewer 2.0](https://colorbrewer2.org/) is the canonical reference for cartography and applies cleanly to charts and slides).

Two accessibility constraints that should not be negotiable. Roughly 8% of men and 0.5% of women have red-green color blindness. Slides that distinguish only by red versus green fail for a meaningful slice of any audience. Distinguish by color *and* a second channel (shape, label, position) for any distinction that matters.

### Visual hierarchy — and the 2:1 size rule

When the eye lands on a slide, it does not start by reading. It scans for contrast. Three forces produce contrast gradients: **size**, **weight**, and **proximity**. The element with the most size and weight wins the one-second scan, regardless of whether you intended it to.

A practical rule from Robert Bringhurst's *The Elements of Typographic Style* (Hartley & Marks, 4th ed. 2013): for display typography — and projection is display typography — size ratios between hierarchical elements should approach **2:1**. A title at 44 pt over body at 22 pt is 2:1 and visible at the back of the room. A title at 32 pt over body at 28 pt is a 1.14× ratio — invisible at projection distance.

A note of honesty about this rule: Bringhurst derived it from the modular scales of book typography, applied to display. I am not aware of a controlled study that has measured 1.5:1 versus 2:1 versus 2.5:1 ratios for instructional slides at projection distance and reported the comprehension difference. The 2:1 rule is craft-derived, not empirically tight. It works in practice; the empirical case for the exact ratio is missing from the literature I can name.

### The textbook-figure-in-projection problem

A textbook figure is engineered for a reading distance of about 14 inches, on a printed page roughly 8 inches wide, with continuous lighting and a caption directly beneath. A projected slide is read at 15–50 feet, on a screen 6–12 feet wide, in variable lighting, often with the caption far from the figure if it appears at all.

The implications: type that is legible in a textbook is illegible at projection. Subfigure letters — "(a)", "(b)" — that work on a printed page become unreadable when shrunk to fit a slide. A diagram with five panels at textbook scale becomes a single visual smear at projection.

The teacher who pastes a textbook figure unchanged into a slide is doing what feels like a faithful citation and is in fact producing a slide the audience cannot read. The fix is not to cite the figure. The fix is to **rebuild the figure for the new reading condition** — one panel, larger type, fewer labels, the caption integrated into the slide body or speaker notes. AI slide generators that ingest a textbook PDF and produce slides routinely embed figures designed for print into projection contexts. The teacher must either reject these figures or prompt for a rebuild.

## 5. The five-question diagnostic

Here is the deck-level audit. Thirty seconds per slide, five minutes for a ten-slide deck. Print it. Tape it above your desk. Run it before any AI-generated deck goes live.

```
THE FIVE-QUESTION SLIDE DIAGNOSTIC
(30 seconds per slide; 5 minutes for a 10-slide deck)

1. Is the headline a claim or a topic label?
   — If label: rewrite as an assertion the slide's evidence supports.

2. Would this slide teach the content without a speaker?
   — If yes: it is a study document, too dense for live use.
   — Move prose to notes; sparsen the slide body.

3. Is there a sentence on this slide I'm about to say aloud?
   — If yes: Mayer Redundancy. Move to notes.

4. Does the visual form match the content structure?
   — Bullets for a list; table for a comparison;
   — diagram for a process; chart for a quantity.
   — If mismatched: rebuild the form.

5. Is every color encoding something?
   — Count categories; count colors.
   — If colors exceed categories: remove the excess.
```

The diagnostic is the phase gate. AI generates the deck; the teacher runs the five questions; only what survives the diagnostic ships. The misuse to flag is applying the diagnostic to *one slide* and stopping. The deck-level pass catches structural failures — repeated topic labels, repeated redundancy — that slide-by-slide review misses.

## 6. Worked example — running the diagnostic on an AI slide

Take a slide the way most AI slide generators ship it. The teacher prompted: *Make a slide on oxidative phosphorylation for AP Biology.* The tool returned the following.

### Before

> **Slide title:** Oxidative Phosphorylation
>
> **Body:**
> - Oxidative phosphorylation is the metabolic pathway in which the mitochondrion uses enzymes to oxidize nutrients, releasing chemical energy used to produce ATP.
> - The proton gradient established across the inner mitochondrial membrane drives ATP synthase, generating approximately 32 ATP per glucose molecule.
> - This process, discovered by Peter Mitchell in 1961 (Nobel Prize, 1978), accounts for the majority of ATP produced during aerobic respiration in eukaryotic cells.
>
> **Visual:** Stock photo of a mitochondrion (cropped from a microscopy image, low contrast).
>
> **Color accents:** Three different colors on the three bullets — blue, orange, green.
>
> **Notes field:** Empty.

Eighty-seven words on the slide. Reading time about twenty-five seconds.

### Running the five questions

1. **Headline a claim or a topic label?** *"Oxidative Phosphorylation"* — a topic label. Fail.
2. **Would this slide teach without a speaker?** No — there is no diagram of the mechanism, no labels on the membrane, no arrow showing what flows where. But it is also too dense to lecture from cleanly. It is the worst of both: too dense for live use, too poorly structured for study. Fail.
3. **A sentence on the slide I'm about to say aloud?** Yes — every bullet is a sentence the teacher will deliver. The Mitchell-1978 aside is a sentence the teacher will say. Redundancy collision in three places. Fail.
4. **Visual form matches content structure?** No. Oxidative phosphorylation is a process — directional, mechanistic, spatial. A bulleted list is a list. Wrong form. Fail.
5. **Every color encoding something?** No. Three bullets, three colors, no encoding relationship between color and content. Pure decoration. Fail.

Five-for-five. The slide is a slideument with a topic label, redundant prose, the wrong visual form, decorative color, and a stock-photo seductive detail thrown in.

### The follow-up prompt

```
SLIDE REVISION PROMPT (built on Chapter 3's role/context/task/constraints)

ROLE: You are a slide designer working in the assertion-evidence tradition
(Alley) and the multimedia-learning tradition (Mayer).

CONTEXT: This is a teaching slide for AP Biology, on oxidative phosphorylation.
The speaker will lecture for about 90 seconds on this slide. Students must be
able to study from the notes field if they miss class.

TASK: Rewrite the slide as a live-deck slide.

CONSTRAINTS:
1. Headline: a single sentence (<=12 words) asserting the slide's claim.
   Not a topic label. Testable against the slide's evidence.
2. Body: at most one visual element — a diagram, labeled image, or short
   equation. No prose bullets. No stock photos.
3. Notes field: the full prose explanation a student studying alone would
   need. Include the Mitchell-1961 reference under "Background."
4. Color: only colors that encode (category or quantity).
   No decorative accents.
5. Output the slide + the notes field as two distinct sections.
```

### After

> **Slide title (headline):** *The proton gradient powers ATP synthase.*
>
> **Body:** A diagram of the inner mitochondrial membrane. H⁺ ions queued on one side; ATP synthase rendered as a labeled rotor; an arrow showing H⁺ flow through the rotor; output labeled "~32 ATP / glucose." Two colors: one for the proton-flow path (the slide's emphasis), one default neutral for the membrane and labels.
>
> **Notes field:**
> "Oxidative phosphorylation is the final stage of aerobic respiration and the high-yield ATP-producing step. The electron transport chain pumps protons across the inner mitochondrial membrane, building an electrochemical gradient. Protons flow back through ATP synthase — a rotary enzyme — and that flow drives the phosphorylation of ADP to ATP. Approximately 32 ATP per glucose molecule are produced this way, compared with 2 net ATP from glycolysis. **Background:** Peter Mitchell proposed chemiosmosis in 1961 and received the Nobel Prize in 1978."

### Lesson and limit

The lesson: the rewrite required no new content. It required deciding what each region of the slide was *for* — headline carries the claim, body carries the visual, notes field carries the explanation — and prompting the tool to honor that division.

The limit: I cannot promise the AI rebuilds the diagram correctly on the first try. AI image generation for technical diagrams is uneven. The honest workflow is to prompt for the diagram description in words, build the diagram in your slide tool (or with a structured AI image prompt — see the Figure Architect approach if you have it), and verify each label by hand. The teacher remains responsible for content accuracy. The phase gate from Chapter 2 — *content accuracy gate* — still applies: every AI-generated material is reviewed for errors before it reaches students.

## 7. Common misconceptions

Four misconceptions are worth disarming before they wreck a deck.

### "AI makes good slides."

What AI makes is *plausible* slides — slides that look like what slides look like, because the training data is full of slides. The training data is also full of slideuments. The model has learned the surface of "what a complete slide looks like." It has not learned the function — *does this slide help an audience learn this content with this speaker in this room?* No model in 2026 has access to those facts. The teacher does. The diagnostic vocabulary is what converts the teacher's contextual knowledge into a prompt the model can execute.

This is the chapter's instantiation of the larger argument from Chapter 2: *AI proposes, instructor disposes.* Generation is cheap. The diagnostic call — *the headline is a label; the body is redundant; the color encodes nothing* — is what the human brings. Cede the diagnostic call to the tool and you get the 11:42 p.m. deck.

### "More on the slide is more for the learner."

False, and the experiment that nails it has been replicated for two decades. Moreno and Mayer (2002) — narration + animation outperformed narration + animation + redundant text on retention *and* transfer. More on the slide produced *less* learning, because the redundant text degraded the spoken explanation. The intuition that "more is more" comes from imagining the silent student studying alone. That student is best served by the notes-field export, which is a separate artifact from the live deck. The live deck is for the live audience. Two artifacts. One source file. One decision the author has to make about which artifact they are producing in any given moment.

### "Bullets are neutral."

Bullets are not neutral; bullets are a *form*, and form makes a claim about content structure. A bulleted list says: *these are items in a set; they have no temporal, causal, or comparative relationship to each other beyond co-membership.* When the content is actually a comparison (a 2×2), a process (a diagram with arrows), a causal chain (a flowchart), or a distribution (a chart), the bulleted list is the wrong form, and the audience has to translate the form before they can read the content. Cleveland and McGill ranked visual encodings; bullets do not appear in the ranking because bullets are not a perceptual encoding — they are a list. The default-to-bullets reflex is the slide editor's bias, not yours.

### "Color makes it pop."

Color makes it *busy*. Color earns its place when it encodes something — a category, a quantity, a direction of comparison. Color spent on decoration is visual bandwidth taken from the slide's actual claims, and your audience has a limited amount of bandwidth per slide. The discipline is to count categories, count colors, and remove the excess. Two colors doing real encoding work outperform six colors doing none.

## 8. The three prompts to keep

These three prompt templates are the ones you will reuse. Each is built on the four-component structure from Chapter 3: role, context, task, constraints.

### Prompt template 1 — Slide revision (slideument → speaker's anchor)

```
ROLE: You are a slide designer working in the assertion-evidence
tradition (Alley, "The Craft of Scientific Presentations") and the
multimedia-learning tradition (Mayer, "Cambridge Handbook of
Multimedia Learning").

CONTEXT: I am revising one slide for live lecture. The audience will
hear me speak for about [N] seconds on this slide. Students who miss
class should be able to study from the notes field. The slide content
is: [paste current slide].

TASK: Rewrite this slide as a live-deck slide.

CONSTRAINTS:
1. Headline: one full sentence asserting the slide's claim,
   <=12 words. Not a topic label. Testable against the evidence.
2. Body: one visual element only (diagram, labeled image, chart,
   short equation). No prose bullets. No stock photos. No
   decorative graphics.
3. Notes field: the full prose explanation a student studying
   alone would need. Include any "interesting but not essential"
   details under "Background."
4. Color: only categorical, sequential, or diverging encoding.
   No decoration.
5. Output: slide + notes as two clearly labeled sections.
```

### Prompt template 2 — Assertion headline rewriter

```
ROLE: You are an editor in the assertion-evidence tradition.

CONTEXT: I have a list of slide titles that are currently topic
labels. I need them rewritten as claim headlines for a [grade level
+ subject] audience.

TASK: For each topic label below, write a claim headline that
(a) is a complete sentence,
(b) is <=12 words,
(c) is testable against typical evidence on that topic,
(d) names what the audience should believe by the end of the slide.

CONSTRAINTS:
- If a topic label cannot be rewritten as a claim without seeing
  the slide's evidence, flag it and ask what evidence the slide
  shows.
- Do not invent evidence the slide may not contain.

INPUT:
[paste topic labels, one per line]
```

### Prompt template 3 — Full deck diagnostic

```
ROLE: You are running the five-question slide diagnostic
(from Chapter 8 of "AI for Teachers: A Practitioner's Guide").

CONTEXT: I am pasting a deck of [N] slides. The audience is
[grade + subject]. Each slide will receive 30–90 seconds of
spoken explanation.

TASK: For each slide, answer the five questions:
1. Is the headline a claim or a topic label?
2. Would this slide teach without a speaker?
3. Is there a sentence on this slide the speaker is about
   to say aloud?
4. Does the visual form match the content structure?
5. Is every color encoding something?

For each FAIL, name the specific failure mode (redundancy,
seductive detail, density, wrong visual form, decorative color)
and propose the specific fix in <=20 words.

CONSTRAINTS:
- Do not rewrite any slide unless I follow up with a revision
  prompt.
- Do not soften failures. A topic label is a topic label.
- Output as a numbered list, one entry per slide.

INPUT:
[paste deck content here, or share file]
```

The first prompt is for one slide at a time. The second is for batch-rewriting headlines (often the fastest single improvement to an AI deck). The third audits the whole deck and produces the punch list. Run the third, then the first on each FAIL, in priority order.

## 9. Exercises

### Exercise 1 — Run the diagnostic (Apply)

Take a deck you currently use — one you built yourself or one an AI tool generated for you. Take the first ten slides. Run the five-question diagnostic on each slide. Record the time taken. Note your three highest-priority failures (the ones that fail the most questions, or fail in the most damaging way).

Acceptance criteria: the audit takes under five minutes for ten slides; you have named three specific failures with their specific principles (redundancy, seductive detail, density, wrong visual form, decorative color).

### Exercise 2 — Rewrite five topic labels as claim headlines (Create)

From any deck you have on hand, list five slide titles that are currently topic labels — "Introduction," "Methods," "Three Causes," "Background," "Key Concept" — and rewrite each as a claim headline. Apply the constraints from Prompt Template 2: complete sentence, 12 words or fewer, testable against the slide's evidence.

Acceptance criteria: each rewrite is a sentence; each is something the audience could agree or disagree with after seeing the evidence; none are filing-system labels.

### Exercise 3 — Write a targeted follow-up prompt (Apply)

Take the highest-priority failure from Exercise 1. Write a follow-up prompt that names the specific failure and the specific fix. Use Prompt Template 1 as a starting point. Run the prompt on your AI tool of choice. Compare the before and after against the five-question diagnostic.

Acceptance criteria: the rewritten slide passes the question that previously failed; no new failures have been introduced; the time from prompt to passing slide is under ten minutes.

### Exercise 4 — Produce a slide + notes export pair (Apply, optional)

Take one slide from your current deck. Apply Prompt Template 1. Verify that the slide functions as a sparse speaker's anchor and the notes field functions as a standalone study explanation. Export the notes as a separate document. Read the document as if you had missed the lecture. Does it work?

Acceptance criteria: the slide works in projection without the notes; the notes work for the absent student without the slide.

## 10. What would change my mind

A controlled study finding no significant difference in learning outcomes between AI-generated decks accepted as-is and AI-generated decks revised against the five-question diagnostic. The current empirical case rests on Mayer's redundancy work (sound), Alley's assertion-evidence work (sound), and the structural argument that AI slide generators reproduce slideument patterns from their training distribution (plausible but not empirically tested for slide-learning outcomes specifically). A null result on the diagnostic-vs-no-diagnostic comparison would weaken this chapter's prescription substantially.

## Still puzzling

- The 2:1 size-ratio rule is craft-derived from Bringhurst's modular scales. A controlled empirical test (1.5:1 vs. 2:1 vs. 2.5:1 at projection distance, comprehension measured) does not exist in the literature I can name. The rule works; the exact ratio is unproven.
- The redundancy-reversal condition (Yue, Bjork & Bjork 2013): when does brief on-screen text *help* the audience by anchoring the spoken stream rather than competing with it? The default case is well-established; the boundary conditions are not.
- Whether AI slide generators trained after 2026 will continue to reproduce slideument defaults, or whether tool-builders will internalize assertion-evidence as the standard. The architecture of the underlying model does not select for pedagogy; the training data does. If teachers start producing better decks at scale, the next generation of tools will be trained on them.
- Empirical research on AI-generated slide *learning outcomes* (Gamma vs. teacher-built decks, NotebookLM vs. PowerPoint defaults) is essentially absent. This is a publishable research question.

## Bridge to Chapter 9

Slides communicate to rooms. Chapter 9 addresses a related but different visual problem: communicating data honestly. The five-question diagnostic in this chapter generalizes — every claim on a slide is a claim about something, and when the claim is quantitative, the visualization carries the burden of proof. Chapter 9 introduces a parallel five-question checklist for charts (truncated y-axes, proportional ink, honest titles, comparison framing). The vocabulary compounds: a chapter on slides plus a chapter on charts gives you the diagnostic eye for almost every visual artifact you produce as a teacher.
